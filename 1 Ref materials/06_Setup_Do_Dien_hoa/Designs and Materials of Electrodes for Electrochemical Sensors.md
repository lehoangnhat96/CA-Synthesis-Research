## Designs and Materials of Electrodes for Electrochemical Sensors 

Pierre J. Obeid, Nouha Sari-Chmayssem, Paolo Yammine, Doris Homsi, Hanna El-Nakat, Zeinab Matar, Soumaya Hamieh, Diala Koumeir, and Ayman Chmayssem* 

Electrode material selection and structural designs of electrochemical chips are fundamental parameters in the field of electrochemical sensing. These parameters directly affect sensor conductivity, selectivity, stability, surface area, and overall performance. This article summarizes the most common electrode architectures and commercially available materials currently used in the development of electrochemical sensors, including carbonbased materials (e.g., boron-doped diamond, graphite, graphene, glassy carbon, carbon nanotubes, and carbon fibers), metal-based 

materials and alloys (e.g., gold, platinum, silver, nickel, and metal oxides), conductive polymers (e.g., polyaniline, polypyrrole, and poly(3,4-ethylenedioxythiophene)), and redox dyes and mediators (Prussian blue, Meldola blue, etc.). It highlights the advantages of each category and identifies suitable electrode materials for specific target analytes. Finally, this review aims to guide readers in selecting appropriate electrode materials and designs tailored to a specific application.

## 2. Design of Electrodes in Electrochemical Sensory 

To design electrodes for electrochemical sensors, several parameters must be considered followed by selecting the appropriate electrode materials. In this part, we describe the key parameters to consider when designing the electrochemical sensors and (bio) sensors (Figure 1). These include the number of electrodes, the substrate material, the surface area of each electrode according to its function, the detection strategy (single mode detection, multiparametric detection, etc.), and the manufacturing process with a particular focus on miniaturized systems such as screenprinted electrodes (SPEs) (Figure 1b). 

Regarding the format of electrodes in electrochemical sensors, it can vary significantly depending on the application and the performance requirements. Common formats include planar electrodes, various types of printed electrodes such as 3D-printed,[[18]] inkjet-printed,[[16]] and SPEs,[[19]] laser-engraved,[[20]] microelectrode arrays,[[21,22]] interdigitated electrodes,[[23]] and 

needle-based electrodes.[[24]] Typically, conventional electrochemical cell configurations involve a two- or three-electrode systems as described at the beginning of this section (WE, CE, and RF) all set in standard (classical) electrochemical cells. These cells conventionally have volume of tens of milliliters adjusted according to the sample’s volume and the intended experiment (Figure 1a). We can also distinguish between classical cells and thermostatic cells, which allow temperature control of the electrolyte using a jacketed setup connected to a water circulation system. 

In this section, we will focus on miniaturized systems, particularly SPEs, as they represent a widely adopted, versatile, and cost-effective platform for both research and commercial electrochemical sensing applications. These electrodes are integrated on the same substrate, offering miniaturized and portable systems (Figure 1b). Common SPE configurations use carbon-based inks for CEs and Ag/AgCl for Res, whereas different in functionalized materials (ink-based) are commonly used for WEs and will be the subject of the next part of the article. SPEs typically use specific cells and may be also integrated to a microfluidic setup. The latter allows experiments to be performed under flow conditions which are more suitable for specific applications (organ-on-a-chip, lab-on-a-chip, etc.) (Figure 1c).[[6,25][–][29]] 

It is important to highlight that an analyte can be detected using a single working electrode (WE) (Figure 2a,b,c,d,i,k).

Electrochemical sensor configurations typically involve two categories: 1) two-electrode system and 2) three-electrode system.[[6,25]] Each design has its own advantages and limitations and is selected based on the intended application and measurement requirements. In the two-electrode system, the WE serves as the site of the electrochemical reaction, where the counter electrode (CE) that completes the circuit also serves as a pseudo-reference electrode (RE) (Figure 2a,b). In this configuration, the CE helps in fixing the applied potential difference.[[26]] The main limitation of this system lies in its poor potential control where it is difficult in absence of true RE to maintain stable potential over time.[[27]] As a result, it is used in simpler systems, mainly as a single-use or disposable low-cost sensors. The three-electrode system consists of three independent electrodes: the WE, the CE, and the RE that maintains a stable and well-known potential (e.g., Ag/AgCl or calomel).[[28,29]] This system is more appropriate for applications that require stable potentials over time making it ideal for sensitive and accurate electrochemical investigations. This system typically includes a dedicated CE typically made of inert or conductive materials such as platinum or carbon. Furthermore, the geometry and surface area of electrodes impact current distribution and mass transport within the cell. 

From a theoretical standpoint, the choice of electrode materials and surface structure significantly affects electron transfer kinetics and overpotential.[[37]] Thus, it is highly recommended that the surface area of the CE be at least three times larger than that of a single WE or the sum of all WEs in a multielectrode configuration. This ensures uniform current distribution and minimize potential drop across electrochemical cell,[[38,39]] helps to maintain a stable electrochemical environment, and ensures accurate control of the WE potential.[[40]] Conversely, high surface area electrodes can increase capacitive background, potentially obscuring faradic signals. Thus, the WE area should be selected based on the experiment purpose. For kinetic studies, the area typically ranges from 10[�][4] to 10[�][6] cm[2] corresponding to electrode diameters of tens of μm (microelectrodes). For electroanalytical studies, larger surface area electrodes are commonly used, ranging from 0.01 to 0.1 cm[2] corresponding to electrode diameters of �1�4 mm (small disk electrodes). In contrast, the RE does not require a large surface area since its primary role is to provide stable and accurate potentials.[[5]] Regarding the distance between electrodes, the separation between RE and CE is generally not critical; however, the distance between the WE and RE separation should be kept small typically between 1�3 mm to minimize the ohmic drop. On the other hand, the WE–CE separation should be larger (5�15 mm) to allow proper current flow and to avoid interference from the CE reactions.[[41]] Finally, some applications require miniaturization in order to integrate the entire electrochemical cell within a specific geometric area (e.g., for portable or point-of-care devices). This step must consider the previously mentioned requirements to ensure the proper design of the electrochemical chip.[[16,18][–][24]] 

SPEs have rapidly emerged since their introduction to the market generating growing interest in the development of electrochemical (bio)sensor platforms. These devices incorporate all the electrochemical cell including the necessary electrodes (WE, CE, and RE) onto the same electrochemical chip 

(Figure 1b and Figure 2). Their ease of mass production and customization, low cost, and availability make them ideal, accurate, and cost-effective tools for (bio)sensor development.[[42]] In addition, the straightforward integration of these sensors into microfluidic systems (microfluidic cells) enables real-time monitoring of target species under controlled microfluidic conditions (Figure 1c and Figure 2i,k). These systems are typically used for the development of lab-on-chip and point-of-care devices.[[43]] Regarding the substrate material of the electrochemical chip, solid materials such as silicon, glass, plastic, and ceramic are typically used for classical application. However, flexible substrate materials such as Kapton (polyimide), hydrogel, polyethylene terephthalate (PET), polydimethylsiloxane (PDMS), and paper-based and textile-based materials are typically used for application that require flexibility and relatively stretchable properties such as wearables (bio)sensors.[[44,45]] Some materials are optically transparent or relatively transparent; these are particularly interesting for devices that combine electrochemical and optical sensing.[[46]] Glass, indium tin oxide (ITO), and PET materials are typically used in applications that require relative transparency combined with the needed electrochemical properties. 

In addition to SPEs, 3D-printed electrodes are gaining attention as customizable and adaptable alternatives. Using additive manufacturing, electrodes can be fabricated with tailored geometries, controlled porosity, and material-specific properties. This allows for a rapid prototyping and integration into wearable and point-of-care devices.[[47]] Likewise, laser-engraved electrodes provide a fast, mask- and solvent-free method to create highly porous conductive carbon-based structures on flexible substrate such as polyimide.[[20,48]] These emerging technologies enable progress across diverse applications particularly in portable, miniaturized, and flexible sensing platforms. 

Finally, some applications have specific requirements that can be challenging to meet. For example, organ-on-a-chip applications equipped with electrochemical (bio)sensors that come in contact with culture media require a sterilization step prior to integration or before the experiment starts.[[49,50]] This implies that the materials used in the construction of the electrochemical chip must supports the sterilization conditions, which can be sometimes challenging and may affect the material’s properties. Another important consideration is the biocompatibility of the materials used. In certain applications, the electrochemical cell may be in direct contact with the target system (e.g., cells, organs, organoids, and interstitial fluid).[[51]] This implies that the materials do not induce any adverse effects or cytotoxic responses.[[52]] So, according to the target application, a specifications list should be considered. To summarize, the previously mentioned requirements and conditions must be applied and respected to ensure proper design of the electrochemical chip and to obtain optimal electrochemical responses.

## 3.1. Primary Sensing Materials 

## 3.1.1. Carbon-Based Materials 

Carbon-based materials are widely used in electrochemical sensors due to their chemical stability, broad potential window, low background current, and analytical versatility. Unlike metal electrodes, they offer longer lifespans and better performance.[[54]] 

Nanostructured forms like graphene, graphite, and carbon nanotubes (CNTs) exhibit superior electrochemical properties, enabling highly sensitive and selective sensing, even in harsh conditions and across wide temperature and dynamic ranges. Meanwhile, boron-doped diamond (BDD) material exhibits a large potential window, making it a suitable for the detection of analytes at high potentials without interference from solvent oxidation/reduction peaks. Graphene and CNTs are distinct carbon polymorphs that have been extensively utilized as electrode materials in electrochemical sensing applications in recent years. Moreover, at room temperature, these carbon nanostructures offer high electrical conductivity, exceptional electron mobility, and a large surface area-to-volume ratio, further enhancing their performance in sensing applications.[[55]] Each carbon-based material will be explored separately in this section to highlight its strengths and limitations (Figure 4). 

## 3.1.1.1. BDD 

BDD has attracted attention due to its remarkable electrochemical properties. It is mainly synthesized using microwave plasmaassisted chemical vapor deposition (MPCVD) at relatively high temperatures, with methane and hydrogen in the presence of diborane as the boron source.[[56]] Alternatives for diborane such as trimethylboron (TMB)[[57]] and boron trifluoride (BF3)[[58]] have also been used, offering good doping efficiencies. While microwave-assisted CVD remains the dominant method for synthetizing high-quality BDD, alternative techniques such as hot-filament CVD and DC arc discharge have been also reported.[[56]] These methods offer varying trade-offs in terms of film uniformity, doping control, and scalability. Under these conditions, sp[3] carbon hybridization predominates, while sp[2] content remains low.[[59]] The sp[3] /sp[2] ratio of BDD films can significantly affect the sensing performance; for example, higher levels of sp[2] -hybridized carbon in BDD are associated with faster electron transfer, an increased background current, and enhanced susceptibility to corrosion,[[60,61]] oxygen reduction,[[62]] and wastewater treatment.[[63]] Boron promotes diamond nucleation during growth and is associated with materials that exhibit smaller crystallite sizes.[[64]] 

Compared to many conventional electrode materials, such as platinum, gold, or graphitic carbon, BDD exhibits similar properties but with notable advantages. BDD is a highly promising electrode material for electrochemical sensor development. It offers exceptional electrochemical and physicochemical properties including a wide electrochemical potential window in aqueous solutions (3 V), high tolerance to extreme potential, high hardness, excellent wear resistance, very low oxygen sensitivity, and enhanced resistance to fouling compared to conventional materials, allowing access to a broader range of redox species and very low background currents.[[65][–][67]] The capacitive current measured within this electrochemical window is significantly lower. In fact, the observed capacitive current is a few nanoamperes per square centimeter (nA cm[�][2] ) on BDD, compared to several tens or even hundreds of microamperes per square centimeter (μA cm[�][2] ) on conventional materials like platinum (Pt)

While BDD electrodes are widely used and effective, they present certain limitations that hinder their large-scale application. First of all, their synthesis presents a significant limitation: the columnar growth process requires several hours at a high temperature (>400 °C), and the resulting films are thick (several hundred nanometers), resistive, and rough contributing to biofouling.[[78]] The high synthesis temperature limits the choice of deposition substrates, with silicon being the most commonly used substrate.[[79]] Moreover, depositing BDD on more conductive and cost-effective supports (such as metallic alloys) or even integrating it as part of microelectronic processes is unfeasible. Except through complex transfer techniques,[[80]] it is impossible to integrate BDD on flexible and low-temperature-resistant substrates. 

## 3.1.1.2. Graphite Carbon 

Pencil graphite electrodes (PGEs) have recently emerged as promising alternatives to conventional carbon-based and metallic electrodes in electrochemical applications. Primarily composed of graphite which is the most thermodynamically stable allotrope of carbon, commonly sourced from mineral ores such as coal,[[81]] PGEs offer a unique combination of desirable properties. These include mechanical robustness, chemical inertness, low background current, a wide electrochemical potential window, strong analyte adsorption capacity, and excellent electrical conductivity due to the sp[2][�] hybridized carbon network.[[82,83]] Additionally, PGEs are inexpensive, disposable, and easily modified or miniaturized, making them particularly attractive for a variety of sensing platforms. 

One of the most significant advantages of PGEs over traditional electrodes, such as GC electrodes (GCEs), is their renewable surface. Unlike GCEs, which require labor-intensive polishing

The material composition of pencil leads further supports their use as electrodes. Contrary to their name, pencil leads contain no metallic lead; instead, they are composed of graphite intercalated with clay and other binders.[[95]] While high graphite content generally correlates with increased conductivity, the optimal electrode performance often depends on the specific interaction between the analyte and the graphite/clay matrix. For paper-based sensor configurations, factors such as the ease of depositing graphite material onto cellulose substrates also influence the selection of pencil hardness.[[96]] In recent years, significant progress has been made in enhancing the functionality of PGEs through chemical modification. These modifications include the incorporation of metal and metal oxide nanoparticles, metal complexes, carbon nanostructures, and conductive polymers (CPs). Akanda et al. provided a comprehensive overview of these developments, highlighting the expanding role of PGEs in the design of chemical and biosensors.[[97]] Torrinha et al. also reviewed enzyme-modified PGEs, though their discussion was limited to specific nanomaterial enzyme conjugates.[[98]] Among these advancements, enzyme immobilization onto PGEs has received growing attention for the development of highly sensitive electrochemical biosensors. Since the late 2000s, a wide variety of enzymes including glucose oxidase, alkaline phosphatase, xanthine oxidase, cholesterol oxidase, horseradish peroxidase, urease, alcohol dehydrogenase, laccase, uricase, ascorbic oxidase, 

lipase, glycerol kinase, and glycerol-3-phosphate oxidase, have been immobilized onto PGEs. These enzyme-modified PGEs biosensors offer an attractive combination of low cost, ease of fabrication, and robust analytical performance for applications in clinical diagnostics, food safety monitoring, and environmental analysis.[[83,91,99][–][101]] 

The continued development of effective enzyme immobilization strategies such as physical adsorption, covalent bonding, and entrapment in polymeric matrices has further enhanced the stability and reusability of PGE-based electrochemical biosensors. As a result, PGEs are increasingly recognized as a promising platform for next-generation biosensors, capable of replacing more conventional carbon electrodes in a wide range of analytical applications.[[102]]

## 3.1.1.4. CNTs: Single-Walled or Multiwalled 

Since their emergence in the early 1990s, CNTs have garnered considerable attention across multiple disciplines, including physics,[[116]] chemistry,[[117,118]] and materials science.[[119]] This interest stems from their extraordinary mechanical strength, electrical conductivity, and chemical stability, which are attributes derived from their unique cylindrical structure composed of sp[2] - hybridized carbon atoms arranged in a hexagonal lattice. 

CNTs are primarily classified into two types: single-walled CNTs (SWCNTs) and multiwalled CNTs (MWCNTs). SWCNTs are composed of a single graphene sheet rolled into a seamless cylinder with diameters typically between 1 and 2 nm. In contrast, MWCNTs consist of multiple concentric graphene cylinders, with diameters ranging from 2 to 25 nanometers and interlayer spacings close to 0.34 nm—similar to that of graphite.[[120,121]] The electronic properties of CNTs are heavily influenced by their structural parameters such as chirality and diameter. Depending on these features, CNTs can act as either metallic or semiconducting materials.[[122]] SWCNTs, in particular, demonstrate well-defined quantum behaviors, including single-electron tunneling and Coulomb blockade at low temperatures.[[123][–][126]] CNTs exhibit several nanoscale advantages, such as high surface-to-volume ratios and quantum confinement effects, which contribute to enhanced sensitivity, selectivity, and electrochemical responsiveness.[[127]] These properties have facilitated their widespread use in diverse technological applications, including chemical sensors,[[128][–][131]] catalyst supports,[[132]] energy devices,[[127,128]] and nanoelectronics.[[124]] CNTs offer enhanced electron transfer capabilities as electrochemical electrodes[[133]] at the electrode–solution interface[[134][–][136]] and outperform traditional materials in conductivity and stability. Their use in electrochemical transducers improves the sensitivity and signal-to-noise ratio of enzyme electrodes,[[137,138]] immunosensors,[[139]] and nucleic acid sensors.[[140,141]] They are well-suited for biosensors, supporting protein immobilization while preserving biological activity.[[142]] 

CNT-modified electrodes are particularly well-suited for amperometric biosensors that utilize oxidase and dehydrogenase enzymes. These electrodes enhance the electrochemical detection of key analytes such as nicotinamide adenine dinucleotide (NADH)[[143][–][145]] and hydrogen peroxide,[[146,147]] both critical in enzymatic sensing schemes. CNTs not only amplify biocatalytic reactions but also facilitate electron transfer when configured as vertically aligned arrays or “CNT forests.” In these 

arrangements, CNTs function as nanoscale molecular wires that promote efficient electron communication between enzymes and electrode surfaces.[[148]] Due to their unique structural and electronic properties, CNTs have found widespread application in electrochemical sensors,[[133,149]] actuators,[[133]] and energy storage technologies.[[150]] The electrical behavior of CNTs is fundamentally linked to the nature of their charge carriers. In graphite, the delocalized π-electrons within its hexagonal lattice facilitate semiconducting properties. CNTs, however, exhibit distinct electrical characteristics arising from their π-bonding and quasi-1D structure. This structure confines electron motion, resulting in a quantized density of states and the emergence of a bandgap at the Fermi level. 

The bandgap in CNTs is inversely related to their diameter; larger-diameter CNTs exhibit smaller bandgaps, supporting low-scattering, long-range electron transport over micrometer distances, provided that structural defects are minimal.[[151]] Electron transfer through CNTs is governed by the availability of conducting states, each capable of supporting quantum conductance by transmitting spin-paired electrons or holes. However, charge carrier reflection at the CNT-metal contact interface can impede this transfer, limiting access to conducting states. This interaction introduces a voltage drop at the contact point, although not along the nanotube itself.[[151]] 

A particularly remarkable feature of CNTs is their ability to carry extremely high current densities of up to 10[10] A cm[2] , exceeding that of conventional metals by three to four orders of magnitude.[[152]] Combined with their exceptional mechanical robustness and chemical stability, these traits position CNTs as strong candidates for replacing silicon in future electronic devices. They are also integral to the development of nanoelectromechanical systems (NEMs).[[153]] Owing to their metallic or semiconducting character, individual CNTs can function as both transistors and interconnects. This duality is especially valuable in the context of modern semiconductor technology, where traditional silicon-based devices are approaching their physical scaling limits.[[154]] CNT-based electronics represent a promising pathway toward enhanced device miniaturization and performance optimization, potentially enabling the continued advancement of Moore’s law through novel circuit architectures.[[155]] 

## 3.1.1.5. GC 

GC, also known as vitreous carbon or glass-like carbon, is one of the most commonly used electrodes for electrochemical sensing applications. It can be produced through low temperature pyrolysis at a temperature range of 300 °C–1200 °C, resulting in sp[2] - hybridized carbon arranged in a hexagonal pattern.[[156]] GC is a low-ordered, hard, nongraphitizing, and isotropic carbon. It can be prepared through carbonization of phenol formaldehyde resin.[[157]] Additionally, it can be derived from various renewable sources like the processing of the lignocellulosic biomass of plants and their residues such as corncobs, rice husks, and sugarcane bagasse.[[158]] Other precursors of vegetable origin used for GC production are sucrose, cellulose, and tannin.[[156]]

## 3.1.1.7. Carbon Black (CB) 

CB is a form of amorphous carbon that has a very fine particle size of tens of nanometers. Like many other carbon-based materials, CB is widely used due to its high surface area to volume ratio, high chemical and thermal stability, relatively good electrical conductivity, and low cost.[[173]] In the sensory field, CB is often formulated into a paste or composite material, where it serves as a conductive matrix when mixed with a blinder or a high dielectric permittivity material such as polymers and ionic liquids.[[174]] This formulation enhances both mechanical stability and electrochemical performance, making it suitable for many electrode applications. CB-based electrodes are typically used in low-cost, disposable sensors or as supporting materials for immobilizing enzymes, redox mediators, or nanoparticles to enhance sensitivity and selectivity in the electrochemical measurements.[[175,176]]

## 3.1.2.2. PPy 

Compared to PANI, PPy has similar properties. PPy is electrochemically active due to its reversible redox activity; it facilitates electron transfer processes used for electrochemical sensing applications. Like PANI, PPy is essentially used to functionalize the WE in electrochemical sensors. It facilitates the detection of analytes by providing a conductive surface for electron transfer. PPy can be integrated into electrochemical sensors using various techniques: 1) electrodeposition—[[189]] direct deposition onto electrode surfaces through electrochemical polymerization, forming a conductive film; 2) screen printing—[[190]] PPy inks are printed onto substrates to create sensor patterns, suitable for mass production; and 3) inkjet printing—[[191]] allows for precise deposition of PPy onto substrates, enabling the creation of complex sensor designs.

## 3.1.2.3. PEDOT 

PEDOT exhibits high conductivity, often enhanced by doping agents like PSS making it suitable for various electronic applications. ## 3.2.1.1. Gold 

Gold-based materials including nanoparticles (AuNPs), nanorods, thin films, and composite structures are widely used in electrochemical sensing due to their distinctive physicochemical properties. Gold-based (bio)sensors have been developed for the detection of a wide variety of analytes, including metal, ions, proteins, nucleic acids, microorganism, and disease biomarkers. These systems are used in medical diagnostics (e.g., cancer, COVID-19, and Parkinson’s disease) as well as agriculture and environmental applications.[[211,212]] Common sensing strategies include DNA-functionalized gold nanostructure–based colorimetric assays, electrochemical immunosensors, microfluidicintegrated systems, and interdigitated electrode-based sensors. Fluorescence and electrochemiluminescence approaches using gold nanomaterials have also shown promise for the detection of small biomolecules such as cholesterol,[[213]] hematin,[[214]] and uric acid.[[215]] 

On the other hand, integration of gold-based materials onto the electrode surfaces can be achieved via different methods: 1) electrodeposition, 2) drop casting, and 3) ink-based fabrication (3D printing, inkjet printing, screen-printing, etc.).[[216]] Despite their many advantages, the relatively high cost of gold deposition remains a limiting factor for widespread use in low-cost or large-scale applications. 

## 3.2.1.2. Platinum 

Platinum-based materials are the most used sensing material for electrochemical sensors, because of their good catalytic activity, electronic conductivity, biocompatibility, and chemical stability.[[217]] They have unique properties such as their surface effect, volume effect, quantum size effect, and macroscopic quantum tunneling effect and can be synthesized by various methods to obtain materials with different physical and chemical properties.[[218]] Platinum possesses many of the aforementioned characteristics and has been used as a CE material for decades due to its inertness.[[219]] Many synthesis methods of platinum-based materials exist where the chemical synthesis method accurately controls the size and shape and affords strong surface chemical versatility and high yield.[[218]] Platinum-based material can be also used as a WE and in three ways as electrochemical catalysts to accelerate the reaction of enzymes, as sensor electrodes to enhance electron transfer, and to modify the electrode surface with an enzyme to fix the substrate and maintain enzyme activity. 

Furthermore, Pt nanoparticle-based biosensors have potential applications in the agricultural and environment sectors, for example, detection of pesticide residues particularly phosphate ester compounds in organophosphorus pesticides, with low detection limit, high analyte selectivity, and high universality using Pt nanomaterials as amperometric acetylcholinesterase biosensors (AChE).[[220]] These AChE amperometric biosensors are sensitive, accessible, and cost-effective compared with the traditional methods of chromatography and spectrometry used for detecting the environmental pollution caused by organophosphorus pesticides.[[218]] 

Moreover, Pt NP-based biosensors play an important role in the field of medicine. Pt-based materials have exceptional cellular biocompatibility[[221]] like gold-based materials; however, PtNPs have a greater cytotoxic effect,[[222]] which could be used in cancer therapy since platinum ions can interact with DNA inhibiting its replication.[[223]] Platinum-based materials have also shown antibacterial activity against Gram-positive and Gram-negative pathogens, similar to that of silver-based materials, although silver present greater toxicity than PtNPs, which limits their clinical use.[[224]] Platinum-based biosensors can be used to efficiently and rapidly test various chemical components in body fluids, such as glucose detection, as well as graphene platinum-based hydrogen peroxide biosensors for reactive oxygen species (ROS) detection. PtNP are also highly sensitive for detecting lactic acid, uric acid (its high accumulation can cause shock, dehydration, and kidney diseases), and glutamic acid (its concentration imbalances can cause psychiatric disorders such as Alzheimer’s disease and Parkinson’s disease), along with many carcinogens.[[218]] 

Platinum nanomaterials and biosensors based on platinum are also widely used in the food sector[[225]] (for the detection of lactic acid that exists in many foods, beverages, and fermented products). Moreover, platinum-coated magnetic beads/palladium nanoparticles (Pt/PdNP) are used for the detection of Salmonella in food and fecal samples.

## 3.2.1.3. Silver 

Silver-based materials have been widely applied in electrochemistry because of their ease of preparation, high conductivity, and relative stability.[[228]] Silver is the best electrical conductor of all metals because it has the lowest electrical resistance. Silver wire coated with silver chloride is relatively insensitive to changes in temperature.[[229]] Silver electrodes are considered among the popular REs due to their simple fabrication.[[230]] Its excellent electrical properties, including a reversible reaction and the ability to provide stable and reproducible potential, make silver ideal for applications such as REs in electrochemical experiments and pH electrodes.[[229]] Moreover, the role of silver as an alloying additive is highlighted when reducing the overpotential of the oxygen evolution reaction on lead anodes used in the electrowinning of zinc.[[231]] In addition, the influence of silver on the electrochemical and corrosion properties of Pb–Ca–Sn–Al grid alloy in sulfuric acid solutions at room temperature was investigated by potentiodynamic and potentiostatic methods.[[232]] However, AgCl electrodes face poor biocompatibility which makes this material challenging in contact with biological agents. 

On other hand, silver nanoparticles are among the most frequently used NPs because of their promising catalytic, electronic, chemical, magnetic, optical, and electrochemical properties.[[233]] For the synthesis of AgNPs, chemical and biological methods are applied by reducing Ag[þ] ions to metallic silver (Ag[0] ) using suitable reducing agents. Conventional chemical reducing reagents are often toxic and nonbiocompatible compounds. Thus, biological reducing agents have been tested as biodegradable and benign compounds for the synthesis of AgNPs. Plants, fungi, and bacteria can be used in the synthesis of AgNPs.[[233]] Electrodes with modified AgNPs are known for their increased surface area and superior conductivity compared with unmodified electrodes. 

Accordingly, silver-based materials have been extensively applied in industrial, biomedical, and environmental applications. In industrial applications, they are used as catalysts for reducing bicarbonate to formate and entering in oxygen reduction reactions. By this process, they can be applied for energy storage, the production of synthetic fuels, and clean energy technologies.[[233]] Recently, AgNPs have gained great attention in the biomedical sector. They have been utilized as key chemotherapeutic drugs to treat various types of cancers, such as pancreatic, prostate, breast, ovarian, and blood cancers. In addition, Ag-based electrochemical sensors are considered a promising tool for determining various organic pollutants in water, thus controlling environmental pollution and subsequent water treatment. Like gold, silver-based materials are expensive compared with other common metals. However, they have various uses and are mainly used for antimicrobial and anticancer therapy and applied in the promotion of wound repair and 

bone healing or as the vaccine adjuvants, antidiabetic agent, and biosensors.[[234]] Despite the use of Ag-based materials in biomedical applications, dental materials, biosensors, biotechnology, and agriculture, doping Ag materials with small quantities of a second metal may enhance its efficiency while maintaining biocompatible levels.[[235]] Gold and platinum are excellent candidates as dopants because they have a greater electrochemical potential than silver and higher biocompatibility.[[235]] 

## 3.2.1.4. Nickel 

Nickel nanoparticles (NiNPs) have harvested significant attention in the field of electrochemical sensing due to their unique physical and chemical properties, cost-effectiveness, and versatile applications.[[236]] Their properties, feasibility in sensor applications, and advantages and disadvantages as well as recent developments in electrode designs incorporating NiNPs are discussed in the following. 

NiNPs exhibit several properties that make them suitable for electrochemical sensor applications such as high surface area, excellent electrocatalytic activity, chemical stability, and magnetic properties.[[237]] The nanoscale size of NiNPs provides a large surface-to-volume ratio, enhancing the electroactive area while facilitating electron transfer.[[238]] They can catalyze various redox reactions, making them effective in detecting analytes like glucose, hydrogen peroxide, and cholesterol while demonstrating good stability under alkaline conditions which is beneficial for certain sensing applications.[[239]] In addition, the intrinsic magnetism of the metal allows for easy separation and manipulation in sensor fabrication processes.[[240]] The integration of NiNPs into electrochemical sensors has been extensively explored, demonstrating their feasibility across various applications. Porous nickel electrodes, for example, have been developed for nonenzymatic glucose detection, demonstrating high sensitivity and stability,[[241]] while for insulin sensing, NiNP-modified electrodes have been fabricated for ultrasensitive detection of insulin achieving low detection limits and wide dynamic ranges[[242]] (Figure 6). 

The incorporation of NiNPs into electrode materials offers various advantages. Being more abundant and less expensive than noble metals such as gold, nickel offers cost-effectiveness thus making NiNP-based sensors more economically feasible.[[243]] The ease of fabrication and versatility of NiNPs enable them to be synthesized and integrated into sensors using various methods such as electrodeposition and chemical reduction[[236]] while they can be combined with other nanomaterials such as graphene or CNTs to create composite electrodes with customized properties while leading to improved sensitivity in detecting various analytes.[[244,245]] Despite their benefits, the use of NiNPs in electrochemical sensors poses certain challenges. They are susceptible to oxidation under certain conditions, thus affecting their stability and performance.[[246]] Their properties may be altered further due to the formation of aggregates, which can reduce their effective surface area.[[247]] Moreover, while the metal is generally considered biocompatible, certain concerns may surface regarding its potential toxicity in certain applications.[[248]]

## 3.2.1.5. Palladium 

Owing to their high biocompatibility, exceptional catalytic properties, large surface/volume ratio, electrical conductivity, 

structural robustness, and stability, PdNPs have shown their significant role in materials for the field of electrochemical sensing and are widely used in sensor development.[[252]] They exhibit high catalytic activity, facilitating redox reactions essential for electrochemical sensing.[[253]] Regarding electrical conductivity, they perform well in ensuring efficient electron transfer, thus enhancing sensor sensitivity.[[254]] Moreover, PdNPs are chemically stable in preserving their functionality over extended periods and under various environmental conditions.[[255]] Such exceptional physical and chemical properties have rendered them appropriate for various applications such as the detection of biomolecules, environmental pollutants, and industrial chemicals.[[252,256,257]] Although they have achieved exceptional performance in glucose and reducing gas detection, they suffer from low sensitivity and weak selectivity in various applications.[[252]] 

PdNPs incorporation into electrochemical sensors has been extensively studied while showing their feasibility across various applications.[[258]] More specifically, PdNPs have been exploited in nonenzymatic sensors for the detection of hydrogen peroxide while exhibiting high sensitivity and selectivity.[[259]] They have also been entrapped in nanoporous gold wires to develop sensitive detection of dopamine revealing their potential in neurological diagnostics.[[260,261]] In addition, PdNP-modified carbon spheres combined with molybdenum disulfide have been used for the electrochemical detection of quercetin, a flavonoid with antioxidant properties.[[262]]

## 3.2.1.6. Metal Oxide-Doped Nanoparticles (MOXNPs) 

MOXNPs have emerged as fundamental components in the development of advanced electrochemical sensors. The exceptional physical and chemical properties such as high catalytic activity, surface-to-volume ratio, and tunable electronic properties make them suitable for detecting various analytes ranging from environmental pollutants to biomolecules.[[269]] This section explores the properties, feasibility, advantages, challenges, and recent advancements in the application of MOXNPs in electrochemical sensor technologies. 

The combination of nanostructures and the modification of sensing materials through the integration of metal oxide with other metal particles or other elements have a considerable impact on sensor performance by enhancing their properties, performance, and functional capabilities. This approach helps in the creation of a larger active surface area, increasing the adsorption of targets onto the sensing material, and increasing the sensor selectivity towards the analyte, while decreasing the interference from others. The nanoscale dimensions of MOXNPs provide a large surface area that facilitates the interaction with target analytes while improving sensor sensitivity.[[270,271]] Despite these advantageous, many metal oxides suffer from intrinsically low electrical conductivity. This can lower the electron transfer and reduces sensitivity of electrochemical sensors.[[272]] This limitation makes MOXNPs less adequate as standalone electrode materials. To overcome this limitation, 

MOXNPs are often combined with highly conductive materials such as graphene, CNTs, or metal nanoparticles.[[273]] These composites improve the overall performance of the sensing platform. ## 3.2.2.3. MB’ 

MB’ is particularly interesting in electrocatalytical detection of NADH, a common cofactor in enzymatic reactions. NADH’s direct oxidation at bare electrodes typically occurs at high potentials (around þ 0.6 V vs. Ag/AgCl) which risks fouling the surface. MB’ overcomes this limitation by catalyzing NADH oxidation at significantly lower potentials (at �0.05 V vs. Ag/AgCl), thus preventing electrode stability and enhancing detection sensitivity.[[314]] 

Several studies have focused on developing innovative electrochemical sensors for the detection of NADH using a carbon nanofiber-modified electrode and MB’ as the redox mediator. The method used by Ana Maria Titoiu et al. enables the enzymatic detection of benzaldehyde of benzaldehyde by coupling it with bacterial aldehyde dehydrogenase (ALDH), which produces NADH as a reaction product.[[315]] The generated NADH is then detected electrochemically through its redox interaction with immobilized MB’. Despite these advantages, the system’s longterm stability is compromised by the gradual desorption of MB’ from the electrode surface. This highlights the need for improved strategies for mediator immobilization to enhance durability. 

Another study conducted by Chmayssem et al. aimed to study the evolution of the cell culture media composition by studying the influences of the cell culture media on the response of electrochemical sensors.[[34]] One of the studied sensors for the indirect detection of nitic oxide (NO) used MB’ to monitor nitrite/ nitrate, the oxidation byproducts of NO. 

Furthermore, a sensitive electrochemical sensor for the detection of NADH has been developed using MB’ as a redox mediator adsorbed onto a carbon paste electrode.[[316]] NADH, a key cofactor in numerous enzymatic reactions, is challenging to detect directly due to its high oxidation potential and the risk of electrode surface passivation. This study addresses these limitations by using MB’, a redox-active dye known for its electrocatalytic properties, 

to lower the oxidation potential of NADH and enhance signal stability. The sensor was fabricated by adsorbing MB onto a carbon paste electrode, and electrochemical performance was evaluated using CV and amperometry. The results demonstrate that the modified electrode facilitates the electrocatalytic oxidation of NADH at a substantially lower potential (��0.05 V vs. Ag/AgCl) compared to the �þ0.6 V required on an unmodified electrode. This shift is critical for minimizing signal interference and preventing electrode degradation. To address stability concerns, more robust immobilization strategies for MB’ such as covalent attachment are being considered. Additionally, optimizing electrode architecture and coupling the system with microfluidic platforms may enable miniaturization and on-site diagnostics. 

## 4. Conclusion 

The design and material selection of electrochemical cells play an important role in determining the performance and sensitivity of electrochemical (bio)sensors. With the growing global demands for rapid diagnostics, environmental monitoring, and food safety assurance, the demand for cost-effective and reliable sensors becomes more critical. Advances in electrode configurations, innovative materials, and integrated detection strategies continue the drive progress of the sensory field. Future research should focus on connecting the laboratory innovation with real-word applications closely to ensure that technologies remain accessible and impactful in improving the quality of life worldwide.

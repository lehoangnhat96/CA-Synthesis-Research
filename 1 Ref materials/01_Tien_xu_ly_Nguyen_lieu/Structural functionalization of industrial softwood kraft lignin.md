## A R T I C L E I N F O A B S T R A C T 

Key words: Urea coating was conducted using polylactic acid (PLA) blended with industrial softwood kraft lignin after Lignin applying a dip-coating technique. The lignin was pre-functionalized via esterification that increased coat layer Esterification hydrophobicity or via amination that created an organically bound nitrogen structure. The PLA film reference Mannich reaction had many pores, while the film from the PLA-lignin derivative complex was highly homogeneous and had no Dip coating pores. The coat thickness was generally adjustable by repeating the coating process reaching up to 81% weight Nitrogen fertilizer against the urea core. After coating, urea release in water was largely delayed, 20–30 and 6–10 times as long as that of uncoated urea or PLA-coated urea respectively. The coated urea will be a highly effective nitrogen fertilizer due to the controlled release after coating, the slow release from the organically bound nitrogen structure, and the expectedly extra stability against urease hydrolysis and microorganism nitrification after using the lignin structure. 

## 1. Introduction 

Nitrogen (N) is an essential element for plant growth and reproduction. Currently, many N fertilizers are utilized for the cultivation of crops and forests, and urea is the most widely used form. However, due to its high water solubility, urea is immediately released after application and substantially leaches into ground water. Moreover, urea cannot be directly absorbed by plants. Conversion to plant-available N is necessary before the N fertilizer can be taken up by the plants, eitheras NH4+ or as NO3− after urease hydrolysis or soil microorganism nitrification. However, both conversions are accompanied by further N leachingloss via, offorNOexample,3−. Therefore,ammoniaa largegasquantityevolutionof fromfertilizerNH4N+cannotor waterbe eventually utilized by crops and forests but instead escapes to the environment, causing large economic losses and serious pollution. There is thus a demand for the development of more efficient N fertilizers (Wu et al., 2008)(see (Mengel et al., 2001; Pereira et al., 2015)). 

In principle, the N utilization efficiency could be improved by deand NOcreasing3−thein order to supply N in the manner that is best suited for plantN release and/or suppression of its conversion to NH4+ absorption and periodic physiological growth. Multiple countermeasures have been considered (see (Chien et al., 2009)). In the 

literature, the most commonly applied physical approach has been to process the urea via coating (Chien et al., 2009) or film or membrane encapsulation (Guo et al., 2005; Niu and Li, 2012; Zhang et al., 2014). The coating shell supplies a physical barrier around the urea core against contact with water so that the urea dissolution and release are delayed, and this coated urea is termed controlled release fertilizer (see Ref. (Chien et al., 2009)). Numerous types of synthetic polymers (including instant polymerization (Liang and Liu, 2006)) have been examined as the coating material, but biodegradable or natural polymers are more attractive following the global development trend for environmental preservation. Polylactic acid (PLA) has been reported to be the best polymer among many polymers tested (Devassine et al., 2002). Currently, there are many different coating methods (Kenneth and Allan, 1994a) and techniques (Kenneth and Allan, 1994b) available, but they are usually sophisticated and require special and expensive mechanical equipment, such as fluidized beds and pan coaters (Shipway, 2006). Coating urea with PLA was achieved using the so-called atomizing method, where expensive equipment was used (Papangkorn et al., 2008). A second chemical approach that was reported involves replacing the inorganic urea fertilizer with organically bound N structures, which slows the time for soil conversion into plant-available N, and this organic N structure is termed slow release fertilizer (see (Chien

A third biological approach has been applied to suppress the biological conversion of N-fertilizer via inclusion of chemical additives that possess inhibitory activities into the fertilizer composition, and this compound fertilizer is termed stabilized fertilizer (Chien et al., 2009). Multifunctional slow-release fertilizers have also been subjects of research in the literature (Ni et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2014b; Wang et al., 2012). 

Lignin is the second most abundant bio-renewable and biodegradable polymer in nature. It has therefore been chosen for investigation in modified N fertilizer using different approaches. Following the physical approach, lignin has been used as a mono-component in urea coatings. The coating was administered to prepare a pellet of controlled release fertilizer either via simple co-melting of the lignin and the urea powders, followed by pelletization (Basile, 1997), or via directly coating the lignin onto the surface of the urea pellets using coating equipment (Mulder et al., 2011; Wang et al., 2003). However the coating effects were limited. To improve the effects, lignin has been examined as a second layer over an initial polymer layer including PLA (Kannan et al., 2015). In addition, lignin has also been used for complex coating together with, for example, ethylcellulose (Fernández-pérez et al., 2008), rosin (García et al., 1997), starch(Majeed et al., 2016) or wax (Cui et al., 2013). However, all these applications were in combinations of the specific equipment requirements. Following the chemical approach, lignin was processed into organically bound N fertilizer, i.e., into socalled slow release fertilizer, via lignin ammoxidation (Meier et al., 1994; Radlein, 1995), where the N fertilizer is in the form of different amides, such as formamide, acetamide, benzamide, etc., as well as other organic N structures (Potthast et al., 1996). Following the biological approach, lignin itself is generally known for having inhibitory effects against both urease hydrolysis and soil nitrification (Huang et al., 2003; Peng et al., 2004). These functions are believed to originate from its multi-phenolic or polyphenolic functional groups (Lodhi and Killingbeck, 1980), which are present in large amounts in lignin structures. For example, urease suppression has been reported from a lignin study (Wang et al., 2003). In fact, the ammoxidated lignin also showed function in urease hydrolysis inhibition (Chen et al., 2009). 

The aim of this study is to develop a highly efficient N fertilizer. The work was based on the urea coating approach using PLA as a basic coating material. In combination with PLA, an industrial softwood kraft lignin has been used for complex coating to further improve the coating effect, and for the first time, coating was conducted without using expensive coating equipment but instead was accomplished using a simple and cheap dip-coating technique. Lignin was utilized after structural functionalization by either esterification or the Mannich reaction. The former rendered a lignin derivative with higher hydrophobicity, imparting improved water barrier properties of the coating and thus higher controlled release efficiency. The latter introduced organically bound N onto the lignin derivative structure, becoming itself a slow release N fertilizer. It could also be expected that adding lignin derivatives to the coating composition would impart lignin’s inherent inhibitory effects against both urease hydrolysis and soil nitrification to the coated urea fertilizer. Therefore, by conducting the dip-coating of urea using a PLA-functionalized lignin complex, we demonstrated a simple and cheap coating technique for attaining highly efficient N fertilizer via a single coating that simultaneously supplies physical, chemical and biological effects. 

## 2. Materials and methods 

## 2.1. Materials 

The softwood kraft lignin (SKL) was prepared on the pilot scale from the black liquor collected from industrial kraft cooking of softwood, following the LignoBoost process (Oehman et al., 2006). It was directly utilized without any further refinement. Comprehensive structural characterizations of SKL have been conducted and reported earlier 

(Wang et al., 2017). Commercial urea pellets and palmitic anhydride (90–95%) were purchased from Sigma-Aldrich. Polylactic acid (PLA) fiber (PL80) with a declared molecular mass distribution (Mw) of 140567 g/mol was obtained from Unitika Ltd. (Osaka, Japan). 

## 2.2. Derivatization 

Esterification was accomplished using Ac2O or palmitic anhydride together with pyridine as the solvent and catalyst. Following the procedure reported by Gellerstedt (1992), the acetylation of SKL into acetylated SKL (AcSKL) was conducted using 500 mg SKL and 5 ml Ac2O/pyridine (1:1, v/v) at room temperature for 24 h. Excess anhydride reagent was then decomposed by the addition of water and methanol, followed by evaporation under reduced pressure using first toluene to assist in the removal of the acetic acid that was formed and then methanol to remove residual toluene. The palmitoylation of SKL into palmitoylated SKL (PaSKL) was conducted using 200 mg SKL (∼1 mmol by using 200 g/mol for one lignin aromatic unit), 2 g palmitic anhydride (∼ 4 mmol) and 5 ml pyridine at room temperature for 24 h, followed by reflux for 1 h. Excess anhydride reagent was then decomposed by the addition of water and methanol, followed by filtration and thorough washing with water to a neutral pH, and finally freeze-drying. 

The Mannich reaction of SKL into the product termed ManSKL was conducted according to previous reports (Wang et al., 2017). Briefly, 100 mg SKL (∼0.50 mmol) was dissolved in 5.0 ml dioxane, followed by the addition of 5.5 mmol diethylamine and 5.5 mmol formaldehyde. The mixture was sealed and stirred at 60 °C for 4 h. After the reaction was complete, the solution of products and unconsumed reagents was evaporated under reduced pressure. 

## 2.3. Chemical analysis 

The OH groups of lignin were phosphitylated and quantified by[31] P NMR spectroscopy, following a procedure adopted from the literature (Granata and Argyropoulos, 1995). In detail, a dried lignin sample or its functionalized product (30 mg) was dissolved in a mixture composed of 100 μl pyridine/N,N-dimethylformamide (1:1), 100 μl N-hydroxy-5norbornene-2,3-dicarboxylic acid imide pyridine solution (20 mg ml[−][1] ) as an internal standard (IS) and 100 μl chromium acetylacetonate pyridine solution (5 mg ml[−][1] ) as a relaxation agent. The reagent 2- chloro-4,4,5,5-tetramethyl-1,3,2-dioxaphospholane (100 μl) was then added, the mixture was stirred for 10 min for completion of the phosphitylation reaction, and then 400 μl chloroform-d was added.[31] P NMR spectra were recorded on a Bruker DRX-400 spectrometer (Bruker, Bremen, Germany), and the data were analyzed by the Topspin 3.0 software (Bruker, Bremen, Germany). The content of hydroxyl groups was obtained by integration of the following spectral regions: aliphatic OH (150.0–145.4 ppm), condensed phenolic OH, including syringyl OH, (145.4–140.2 ppm), guaiacyl phenolic OH (140.2–139.0 ppm), p-hydroxyphenyl phenolic OH (136.5–138.6 ppm), and carboxylic OH (136.0–133.6 ppm).

## 3.3. Coating urea 

## 3.3.1. Coating technique 

In this study, PLA was used as the basic material for coating. In the literature, PLA has been used in a chloroform solution for dip-coating on metal material (Shi et al., 2015). Here, we used a similar but less toxic solvent, dichloromethane (DCM). Although dissolution in DCM was very slow, a clear solution of 60% (w/v) concentration was successfully prepared after stirring overnight at room temperature. Since lignin and lignin derivatives are insoluble in DCM, to conduct PLA and lignin complex coating, it was dissolved in dioxane and then mixed with the PLA/DCM solution. Dioxane was found to be well miscible with DCM, such that the mixed DCM-dioxane solution was perfect for conducting dip-coating. 

## 3.3.2. Preparation and evaluation of cast films 

In the dip-coating process, the coating layer is formed in a similar manner to film formation from solution. Therefore, the PLA-lignin complex coating was first evaluated by film casting for coating capacity and coating quality. The film formed from a 60% DCM solution of the basic PLA material is a uniform film, as seen by optical observations (Fig. 6a). However, under SEM analysis, many pores are visible on the nanometer scale (Fig. 6b). Further image analysis revealed that PLA cast film has pores with an average pore size of 410 ± 155 nm. These pinholes will lead to water or steam penetration and then urea dissolution and transport. Apparently, PLA alone could not form a good film. From the PLA and lignin complex in DCM-dioxane solution, unmodified SKL was found to phase-separate from PLA in the cast PLAlignin film (Fig. 6c), forming pores with even larger sizes (1133 ± 553 nm) than those in the PLA film (Fig. 6d). However, after functionalization by esterification, both esterified lignins formed optically homogeneous films with PLA (Fig. 6e and 6g). Under SEM observation, however, AcSKL caused the formation of large-sized chunks (Fig. 6f) with pores in size of 775 ± 348 nm. In contrast, a very homogeneous layer without any visible pores was formed from PaSKL (Fig. 6h). Therefore, palmitoylation functionalization resulted in an excellent reinforcement effect in PLA film formation. On the other hand, after functionalization by the Mannich reaction, a similar perfect reinforced film was formed from the PLA-ManSKL complex; a very homogeneous (Fig. 6i) and pore-free layer was formed (Fig. 6j). The starting SKL has a molecular mass distribution of Mw = 5500 g/mol. After functionalization, both PaSKL and ManSKL still have molecular masses in a similar range. Compared to the PLA, whose Mw is 140567 g/mol according to the manufacturer, these lignin derivatives apparently functioned as a plasticizer for PLA, which improved the film formation and quality. 

The desired function of the coating layer is as a physical barrier to block water. This capability is closely related to the film hydrophobicity, which can be evaluated by contact angle values (Table 2). It could be seen that the original PLA film is somewhat hydrophilic (47.50°). Since lignin is essentially hydrophobic due to the presence of multiple aromatic rings in its structure, the PLA-SKL film becomes obviously more hydrophobic (79.39°). After functionalization, good 

hydrophobicity is achieved by esterification. Due to the presence of longer aliphatic chains in palmitic acid compared with those in acetic acid, a higher hydrophobicity is observed for the PLA-PaSKL film (87.01°) than the PLA-AcSKL film (70.64°). The lower contact angle for PLA-AcSKL film than PLA-SKL film should be due to the formation of large-sized chunks in the former one, which is confirmed by SEM observation as shown in Fig. 6f. After the Mannich reaction, the PLAManSKL film is more hydrophobic (65.52°) than the PLA film (47.50°) but less hydrophobic than the PLA-SKL film (79.39°) since the introduced diethylaminomethyl group (Fig. 2) is somewhat hydrophilic. 

## 3.3.3. Dip-coating results 

Since lignin aggregation (as chunks) was observed in the PLA-AcSKL film, which has also lower contact angle than the PLA-PaSKL film, our dip-coating experiments focused on the PLA-PaSKL and PLA-ManSKL coatings, which represent different types of functionalizations, using a PLA coating for comparison. Generally, the simple dip-coating performance resulted in good attachment of the coating layer on the surface of urea pellets, as shown by the cross-sectional image of a coated urea pellet (Fig. 7a). A typical coated urea pellet is shown in Fig. 7b, and it could be seen that the coating layer is very compact and similar to the cast films. The wall layer is rather homogeneous and well distributed. The coating process was repeated several times, and the quantity of the coating was found to be dependent on the number of coating cycles. This tendency is, in general, irrespective of the type of lignin derivative used (Table 3). 

In fact, the coating layer was so compact and strong that the layer survived after immersion in water and complete release of the urea core (Fig. 7c) (see section 3.4). After complete urea release, which removed the entire urea core, SEM images of the surviving coating layers clearly revealed that the wall thickness is dependent on the number of coating cycles, and no layer separation was found between different coating layers, irrespective of the coating mixture components (examples of the PLA coating are given in Fig. 7d and 7e and the PLA-PaSKL coating are given in Fig. 7f and 7g). In other words, the coating is one single layer, and its quantity or coated wall thickness could be tuned by adjusting the number of dip-coating cycles. To our best knowledge, this is the first time that a single-layer coating using a PLA-lignin blend has been reported. 

Upon calculation, the urea core itself contains 46.6% N. In the PLAlignin complex, 9.1% of the coating mixture was from lignin. Therefore, for the coated urea with the thickest shells, i.e., 51–81% coating (Table 3), the added lignin quantity is 5–8% of the urea weight. When ManSKL is used, since ManSKL contains 2.5% N, the coating shell will contain 0.23% organic N. For the coated urea with the thickest shells, therefore, approximately 0.25-0.40% of the total N will be organically bound N. 

## 3.4. Nitrogen release 

In this study, immersion testing using water, one of the simplest and most commonly used methods in the literature (Chen et al., 2002; Chen et al., 2009; Wang et al., 2014a) and in several national and international standards (see (Carson and Ozores-Hampton, 2012)), was applied to evaluate the release performance of the PLA-lignin complex coated fertilizers. It can be seen from Fig. 8 that the complete release of bare urea took only 50 s (seen also by the disappearance of the urea pellets). The PLA coating delayed the complete release to 150 s, i.e., three times as long as that without the coating. Esterified PaSKL delayed the release to approximately 1500 s, i.e., 10 and 30 times as long as that of the PLA coating and bare urea, respectively. The Mannich reaction product ManSKL also slowed the urea release more than the PLA coating reference, taking approximately 1000 s, or 6.7 times as long. This release is 20 times as long as that of bare urea. Here, when PLA was used alone as the coating material, the urea release delay is due to the physical barrier property of PLA coating. The lignin functionalization conducted in this study could be expected to also influence the later steps in soil. One important effect from the lignin structure is the inhibition of urease hydrolysis and nitrification by soil microorganisms. In the literature, a lignin coating was found to be capable of providing a 12% decrease in the release rate of bare urea in soil and give a higher urea efficiency in corn cultivation due to lignin’s inhibition of nitrification (Peng et al., 2004). The mechanism of lignin’s inhibition of nitrification includes hydrogen-bond formation between lignin hydroxyl groups and ammonium, which is related to the polyphenol structures in lignin (Sabahi et al., 2014). Lignin could also reduce N2O emissions by approximately 83–96%, thus having potential as a fertilizer amendment to improve the N fertilizer efficiency (Huang et al., 2003). In addition, the utilization of lignin for coating urea can lead to many other advantages. For example, the 

hydrophobicity of lignin observed in this study would help prevent PLA from undergoing hydrolysis, as hydrolytic instability is a critical PLA weakness during application (Södergård and Inkinen, 2011). As reviewed by Gul et al. (2014), lignin structures can influence the soil microbial community structure to facilitate the control of soil quality through the reduction of greenhouse gas emissions from soil, the retention of soluble nutrients, the promotion of soil aggregate formation and stabilization, and the bioremediation and detoxification of natural and man-made organic pollutants. 

Generally, several improvements could be made in the future to further slow the release of the coated urea reported in this study. As was found, crack channels (Fig. 7c) are mainly responsible for urea release. More research should be conducted to eliminate these cracks or prevent their formation. This could be tested by controlling the speed of solvent evaporation during the dip-coating procedure or conducting annealing to prevent the formation of cracks and pores (Devassine et al., 2002). ## 4. Conclusions 

Industrial lignin was functionalized by esterification or the Mannich reaction to a ∼100% completion. Using dip-coating, PLA-functionalized lignin complex-coated urea can be simply prepared with adjustable coating thickness and high coating efficiency. The use of functionalized lignin, especially palmitoylated lignin, obviously delays the release of the urea core in water, and utilization of the Mannich reaction-functionalized lignin supplies additional organically bound nitrogen to the fertilizer. Therefore, the coating conducted in this study not only constructs a physical barrier to delay urea dissolution but also supplies chemically slow-release, organically bound nitrogen, and biological stabilization effects, such that the coated urea is a simple but highly efficient fertilizer.

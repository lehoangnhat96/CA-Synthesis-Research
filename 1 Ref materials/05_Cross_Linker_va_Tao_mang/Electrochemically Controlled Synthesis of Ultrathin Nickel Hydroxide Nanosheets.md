## Electrochemically Controlled Synthesis of Ultrathin Nickel Hydroxide Nanosheets for Electrocatalytic Oxygen Evolution 

Li-Ming Cao, Qing-Cai Cao, Jia Zhang, Xuan-Yi Zhu, Rong-Zhi Sun, Zi-Yi Du, and Chun-Ting He*

ABSTRACT: The development of oxygen evolution reaction (OER) catalysts with high activity and high stability through convenient and economical methods is greatly important for the promotion of hydrogen energy based on electrolysis technology. Herein, by using an unconventional high electrodeposition potential, novel petal-like clusters constructed by cross-linking ultrathin nickel hydroxide nanosheets were controllably synthesized on nickel foam (or copper foam or carbon cloth) and the effect of electrodeposition conditions on their OER performance was carefully explored. Due to the abundant catalytically active sites, promoting electron conduction/mass transmission from the specific micro−nano structure, as well as the ultrasmall thickness of ∼3.0 nm, the optimized α-Ni(OH)2/NF self-supporting electrode exhibits excellent electrocatalytic performance for OER, merely requiring low overpotentials of 192 and 240 mV to yield current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH, respectively, which surpassed those of all of the reported nickel hydroxide/oxides and the benchmark RuO2. Moreover, α-Ni(OH)2/NF can drive the high-current density (500−1000 mA cm[−][2] ) OER at low overpotentials, meeting the requirements of potential industrial applications. 

## 1. INTRODUCTION 

The depletion of fossil fuels and growing environmental problems have forced people to seek sustainable and clean energy.[1][,][2] Hydrogen (H2) has the advantages of a high combustion value and no pollution, making it an ideal source of renewable clean energy.[3][−][5] The electrolysis of water to produce H2 not only is simple and practical but also can yield high-purity H2, which is a promising approach for the conversion of renewable energy into hydrogen energy.[6][−][8] Water splitting is composed of two key half-reactions: oxygen evolution reaction (OER) and hydrogen evolution reaction (HER).[9][−][11] Between them, HER has great thermodynamic advantages.[12][,][13] In contrast, OER involves a complex multiproton coupled electron transfer process, resulting in slower oxygen evolution kinetics, which requires a higher overpotential to accelerate the reaction, thus becoming the bottleneck of water splitting technology.[14][−][18] Therefore, it is very important to prepare highly active OER electrocatalysts to decrease the activation energy of the reaction and accelerate the kinetics of oxygen evolution, thereby improving the efficiency of water splitting. Currently, Ir/Ru oxides are recognized as one kind of the most active OER electrocatalysts.[19] However, the scarcity and high cost of these precious metal catalysts have seriously hindered their largescale commercial application. As a consequence, it is particularly important to develop low-cost, available, and highly active non-noble metal catalysts, such as earth-abundant 

transition metal (Fe, Co, Ni, Mn, etc.)-based electrocatalysts.[20][−][23] In particular, cobalt-based electrocatalysts, such as Co-based oxides/hydroxides, sulfides, selenides, phosphides, and borides, have been widely developed for their remarkable OER performance.[24][−][26] For example, Wang et al. synthesized ultrathin CoFe LDH nanosheets by a hydrothermal method, and the catalyst needed an overpotential of only 233 mV to yield a current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH.[24] Compared with cobalt, nickel usually exhibits much weaker OER activity, although it is much more abundant and cheaper.[27] For instance, the OER performances of pure nickel oxide/hydroxide electrocatalysts are generally rather undesirable.[28][−][33] Han et al. synthesized nickel boridehydroxide nanoparticles [F-Ni2B/Ni(OH)x] by a one-step wetchemical method, which required an overpotential of 340 mV to yield a current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH.[28] Liu et al. synthesized Ni(OH)2 nanosheets with a thickness in the range of 20−100 nm by a hydrothermal method, requiring an overpotential of 308 mV to yield a current density of 10 mA

Electrodeposition is a convenient and universal technology that can be used for large-scale industrial production.[34][,][35] It needs simple and low-cost equipment to precisely control the nucleation and growth of substances to prepare electroplated products with different structures and morphologies.[36][,][37] Direct electrodeposition of electrochemically active species on conductive substrates can construct self-supporting electrode materials for supercapacitors, batteries, and electrocatalysis.[38][−][41] Compared with the powdered catalysts, the electrodeposited self-supporting electrodes, on one hand, can avoid the use of expensive conductive polymer adhesives and possess quite abundant and highly exposed electrochemically active sites; on the contrary, they show excellent stability, especially working under high current densities, in which a large number of O2 bubbles are easy to peel off the glued powder catalysts, yet the catalysts directly grown on the conductive substrate are more robust.[42][,][43] Currently, there have been some reports of electrodeposited nickel hydroxide/ nickel oxide self-supporting electrodes for OER, but they are mostly thick nanoplatelets or nanoparticles with unsatisfactory catalytic activities.[44][−][47] For example, Ni et al. prepared nickel hydroxide nanoparticles with a size of ∼20 nm on nickel foam (NF) by electrodeposition, which required an overpotential of 366 mV to yield a current density of 100 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH.[44] Geng et al. synthesized α-Ni(OH)2 nanosheets on a NF/N-CNT substrate via electrodeposition under a constant cathodic current of 2 mA cm[−][2] , which required an overpotential of 254 mV to yield a current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH.[47] In addition, most of the electrodeposition potentials ever used in the synthesis of nickel hydroxides/ nickel oxides do not exceed −1.0 V versus Ag/AgCl.[44][−][47] In fact, the electrodeposition potential plays an important role in the morphology and structure of nanomaterials, and under a high potential or a high current density, the ultrafast ion migrations may lead to special micro−nano structures and/or novel active sites.[41] In addition, the other electrodeposition conditions, such as electrodeposition time, electrolyte concentrations, and anion species, are also significant for the electrochemical in situ synthesis,[41] yet their specific effects on the morphology and structure of nickel hydroxides are rarely studied. 

Herein, we synthesized three-dimensional (3D) petal-like clusters constructed by cross-linking two-dimensional (2D) ultrathin nickel hydroxide nanosheets on NF by using a relatively high electrodeposition potential and carefully explored the effect of electrodeposition conditions on the OER performance of nickel hydroxide. We found that the electrodeposition potential strongly influences the formation of petallike clusters, thereby affecting the catalytic performance of nickel hydroxide. Benefiting from the novel micro−nano structure as well as the rather small thickness of ∼3.0 nm, the obtained nickel hydroxide nanosheets possess abundant catalytically active sites, promoting electron conduction/mass transmission, and remarkable stability. Therefore, the electrodeposited α-Ni(OH)2/NF self-supporting electrode prepared under optimal parameters exhibits excellent electrocatalytic performance for OER, merely requiring low overpotentials of 

192, 240, and 290 mV to yield current densities of 10, 100, and 250 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH, respectively. Moreover, α- Ni(OH)2/NF can even drive the high current density (500− 1000 mA cm[−][2] ) at low overpotentials, indicating that it has potential industrialization prospects. 

## 2. EXPERIMENTAL SECTION 

2.1. Synthesis of Materials. For the synthesis of α-Ni(OH)2 nanosheets on nickel foam (NF, thickness of 1.7 mm), NF was ultrasonically cleaned in ethanol, a 3.0 M HCl solution, and ultrapure water for 10 min. To fabricate α-Ni(OH)2/NF, electrodeposition was performed at different electrodeposition potentials employing a threeelectrode system with a NF working electrode, a Pt counter electrode, and a Ag/AgCl (in a 3 M KCl solution) reference electrode. An aqueous solution of Ni(NO3)2·6H2O was used as the electrolyte. The NF working electrode (1 cm × 3 cm) was immersed in the electrolyte. The detailed experimental process is as follows (take the electrodeposition conditions with the deposition potential of −2.0 V vs Ag/ AgCl, an electrolyte concentration of 0.2 M, and a deposition time of 300 s as the example). First, a 0.2 M Ni(NO3)2·6H2O aqueous solution was poured into the electrolytic cell. Then, the NF working electrode (immersion area of 1 cm × 3 cm), a Pt counter electrode, and a Ag/AgCl (in a 3 M KCl solution) reference electrode were inserted into the electrolyte and then applied to a cathodic deposition at −2.0 V for 300 s. To fabricate α-Ni(OH)2/NF at different electrodeposition potentials, it is only necessary to set the cathode deposition potentials to −0.1, −1.5, −2.5, or 3.0 V, and the other synthesis conditions are the same. To fabricate α-Ni(OH)2/NF at different electrolyte concentrations, it is only necessary to change the electrolyte concentration to 0.1, 0.3, or 0.4 M, and the other synthesis conditions are the same. To fabricate α-Ni(OH)2/NF at different electrodeposition times, it is only necessary to change the electrodeposition time to 200, 400, or 500 s, and the other synthesis conditions are the same. To fabricate α-Ni(OH)2/CF and α- Ni(OH)2/CC, copper foam and carbon cloth are used as the working electrodes, respectively, and the other synthesis conditions are the same. 

2.2. Characterization. Scanning electron microscopy (SEM, ZEISS EVO 10) and transmission electron microscopy (TEM, JEM2100F) were utilized to characterize the morphology of the catalyst. The D8 ADVANCE X-ray diffractometer was used to obtain the powder X-ray diffraction (PXRD) data. The ESCA Lab250 X-ray photoelectron spectrometer was used to obtain the X-ray photoelectron spectroscopy (XPS) data. 

2.3. Electrochemical Measurements. Electrochemical measurements were performed in 1.0 M KOH through a model CHI760E electrochemical workstation employing α-Ni(OH)2/NF, Hg/HgO, and Pt plate as the working, reference, and counter electrodes, respectively. The loading mass of α-Ni(OH)2 on the substrate is 9.6 mg cm[−][2] . For comparison, we loaded commercial RuO2 with the same mass on NF. The homogeneous catalyst ink was prepared by dispersing RuO2 (25 mg) in ethanol (450 μL) and a 5% Nafion solution (50 μL) with ultrasonication. Subsequently, the as-obtained ink (192 μL) was evenly spread on a piece of clean NF. The asprepared α-Ni(OH)2/NF self-supporting electrode was directly utilized as the working electrode. All electrodes were activated to a stable state through cyclic voltammetry (CV) before their electrochemical performance was tested. CV activation was carried out in the sweep range of 1.1−1.8 V versus a reversible hydrogen electrode (RHE) with a scan rate of 100 mV s[−][1] . The electrochemical surface area (ECSA) was evaluated by measuring the electrochemical doublelayer capacitance (Cdl), which was measured by CV measurements in the non-Faradaic region (0.93−1.03 V vs RHE) at scan rates of 60, 80, 100, 120, 140, and 160 mV s[−][1] . A linear relationship between the capacitive currents at 0.98 V versus RHE with the scan rates could be obtained. The Cdl value was calculated, which is half of the linear slope. ## 3. RESULTS AND DISCUSSION 

The commercial NF with excellent electrical conductivity was selected as the substrate to fabricate the α-Ni(OH)2/NF selfsupporting electrode (Figure 1a). α-Ni(OH)2/NF was 

prepared by a simple and convenient electrodeposition method. During the preparation process, the nitrate ions in the Ni(NO3)2·6H2O electrolyte migrated to the vicinity of the NF electrode under the action of an external electric field and were reduced to produce OH[−] .[41] Deposition could occur when the precipitation equilibrium constant of Ni(OH)2 was reached. Therefore, the synthesis mechanism can be expressed as follows: NO3− + H2O + 2e− → 2OH− + NO2− (cathodic reduction) or Ni[2+] + 2OH[−] → Ni(OH)2 (precipitation). To explore the optimal preparation conditions for α-Ni(OH)2/NF with high electrocatalytic performance, we investigated the effects of electrodeposition potential, electrodeposition time, and electrolyte concentration on the OER performance of the catalysts. Subsequently, we explored the effect of electrolyte concentration on OER performance. The results show that the higher the concentration of Ni(NO3)2· 6H2O, the greater the loading mass of α-Ni(OH)2. The loading mass of α-Ni(OH)2 greatly increases from 0.1 to 0.3 M and increases slightly from 0.3 to 0.4 M (Figure 2b). When the loading mass of the catalyst is too low, too few catalytic active sites are provided, which would weaken its catalytic performance for OER. When the loading mass of the catalyst is too high, although it can provide abundant catalytic active sites, it would also hinder mass transfer and charge transfer, thereby weakening its OER catalytic activity. Finally, the effect of electrodeposition time on OER perform-

Galvanostatic electrolysis reveals that α- Ni(OH)2/NF obtained by electrodeposition at 300 s possesses the best OER activity (Figure 2c and Figure S4). An overly low or high loading mass can decrease the catalytic activity. The change in electrodeposition conditions would directly regulate the loading mass of α-Ni(OH)2, which plays a significant role in boosting the catalytic activity of the self-supporting electrode.

To further reveal the root cause of the effect of electrodeposition conditions on OER activity, we tested the electrochemically active specific surface area (ECSA) and conductivity of these self-supporting electrodes. The ECSA was evaluated by measuring the electrochemical double-layer capacitance (Cdl) (Figures S13−S15). The larger the Cdl, the greater the ECSA of the catalyst.[10] As shown in Figure S16, the ECSA of the self-supporting electrode increases with the loading mass of α-Ni(OH)2 and reaches the maximum when the loading mass reaches ∼9.6 mg cm[−][2] , and continuing to increase the loading will decrease the ECSA. In particular, the thinner the α-Ni(OH)2 nanosheets, the larger the ECSA of the catalyst (Figure 2d−f), indicating that the preparation of ultrathin nanosheets is the essential reason for improving the OER activity of the catalyst. High electronic conductivity is also beneficial for enhancing the electrocatalytic activity of the catalyst for OER. EIS was utilized to reveal the charge transfer resistance (RCT) of α-Ni(OH)2/NF. As shown in Figure S17, an appropriate increase in the catalyst loading mass can significantly improve the electron conductivity, but excessive loading will hinder electron conduction. Generally, on one hand, excessive loading increases the thickness of the nanosheets, making the band gap of the material larger.[52][,][53] On the other hand, because nickel hydroxide is far less conductive than metallic nickel, when the loading is excessive, the electron transfer efficiency should be naturally lower.[41] Therefore, we can conclude that accurately regulating the electrodeposition conditions can significantly adjust the ECSA and conductivity of α-Ni(OH)2/NF to finely tune its catalytic activity.

Studies have shown that the active species of some electrocatalysts will change during the OER process.[26] To reveal the real active species for OER, we characterized the composition, morphology, and crystal phase structure of the catalyst after OER. The PXRD pattern of α-Ni(OH)2 after OER is presented in Figure 3a. The diffraction peaks at 19.25°, 33.07°, 38.54°, 52.10°, 59.05°, and 62.73° can be attributed to the (001), (100), (101), (102), (110), and (111) crystal planes, respectively, of the Ni(OH)2 phase (JCPDS Card No. 14-0117), and the diffraction peaks at 12.81°, 37.93°, 51.59°, and 66.23° can be attributed to the (003), (102), (108), and (110) crystal planes, respectively, of the NiOOH phase (JCPDS Card No. 06-0075), suggesting that α-Ni(OH)2 was converted to β-Ni(OH)2 and γ-NiOOH [β-Ni(OH)2/γNiOOH] after OER.[58][,][59] In addition, we also tested the PXRD of the catalyst after galvanostatic electrolysis at 10 mA cm[−][2] for 75 h and found that the two-phase structure [βNi(OH)2/γ-NiOOH] after the initial transformation can still be maintained (Figure S22). The results confirmed that the in situ-generated β-Ni(OH)2/γ-NiOOH was very stable during OER. The SEM and TEM images show that the in situtransformed β-Ni(OH)2/γ-NiOOH maintains the nanosheet morphology (Figure 5a and Figure S23), confirming its remarkable morphological stability during the OER process. Moreover, HRTEM also demonstrated that the crystal structure of α-Ni(OH)2 had changed. As shown in Figure 5b, the clear lattice fringes with lattice spacings of 0.230 and 0.240 nm correspond to the crystal planes of (101) of β- Ni(OH)2 and (102) of γ-NiOOH, respectively.[58] Furthermore, XPS was utilized to reveal the surface chemical composition and element valence state of α-Ni(OH)2 before and after OER. As shown in Figure 5c, the high-resolution Ni 2p XPS spectrum in α-Ni(OH)2 before OER exhibits four characteristic peaks, where the two peaks at 855.6 and 873.4 eV correspond to the Ni 2p3/2 and Ni 2p1/2 features of Ni[2+] , respectively.[60][,][61] The two peaks located at 861.4 and 879.6 eV are attributed to the satellite peaks of Ni[2+] .[58] As shown in Figure 5d, the two characteristic peaks at 531.0 and 532.3 eV can be fitted from the high-resolution O 1s XPS spectrum. Obviously, the characteristic peak located at 530.9 eV can be assigned to the Ni hydroxide species in the α-Ni(OH)2 nanosheets, and the other characteristic peak at 532.1 eV is associated with water molecules adsorbed on the surface of α- Ni(OH)2.[58][,][60][,][61] The analysis presented above confirms that the surface of α-Ni(OH)2 before OER is composed of Ni[2+] and hydroxide species. After OER, in the high-resolution Ni 2p spectrum, there are two spin orbit doublets and two corresponding satellite peaks (Figure 5c). The first doublet at 855.2 and 873.0 eV can be assigned to Ni[2+] in β-Ni(OH)2, which is negatively shifted by ∼0.4 eV compared to that of α- Ni(OH)2.[58][,][62] The second one at 856.3 and 874.1 eV corresponds to Ni[3+] , implying the generation of NiOOH.[58][,][62][,][63] From the high-resolution O 1s spectrum (Figure 5d), the two characteristic peaks at 529.1 and 531.0 eV can be fitted, corresponding to the nickel−oxygen (Ni−O) and the protonated oxygen (H−O), respectively.[58][,][61][−][63] The XPS analysis after OER demonstrates that a portion of Ni(OH)2 was in situ transformed into NiOOH, which has been generally considered to be the truly active species of OER. Many studies have shown that the γ-NiOOH transformed from Ni-based 

precatalysts has many defect sites, such as vacancies, edges, corners, stacking faults, etc., which can act as highly active species to enhance OER catalysis.[22][,][64][−][66] Therefore, this phase transition is beneficial for enhancing the catalytic activity of the catalyst. On one hand, α-Ni(OH)2 is unstable under strong alkaline solutions and can transform easily to the more stable β-Ni(OH)2; on the other hand, part of the nickel hydroxide will be converted into stable γ-NiOOH active species during the OER process.[50][,][64][−][66] Therefore, this phase transition is also beneficial for enhancing the catalytic stability of the catalyst. In addition, the PDOS calculated by density functional theory (DFT) showed that the d orbital center of the Ni atom in ultrathin NiOOH nanosheets moves toward the Fermi level (that is, toward the higher energy level) compared with the bulk state (Figure S24). This is beneficial for enhancing the adsorption of OER intermediates on Ni atoms (such as O*), thereby improving the catalytic activity.[66][−][68] 

To further prove the universality of this electrodeposition method for substrate materials, we also electrodeposited ultrathin nickel hydroxide nanosheets on copper foam (CF) and carbon cloth (CC) conductive substrates under the same conditions (Figure S25). As shown in Figures S26−S28, the crystal structure and morphology of nickel hydroxide electrodeposited on CF and CC are the same as those on NF. The asobtained α-Ni(OH)2/CF and α-Ni(OH)2/CC self-standing electrodes also exhibit outstanding electrocatalytic activity and stability for OER. LSV curves reveal that α-Ni(OH)2/CF and α-Ni(OH)2/CC require overpotentials of 278 and 375 mV, respectively, to yield a large current density of 100 mA cm[−][2] (Figure S29). As shown in Figure S30, the Tafel slopes of the α-Ni(OH)2/CF and α-Ni(OH)2/CC electrode are 117 and 135 mV decade[−][1] , respectively, suggesting their good catalytic kinetics for OER. To avoid the interference from the oxidation peaks of Ni[2+] to Ni[3+] , galvanostatic electrolysis measurements were carried out to determine the overpotentials at the current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] . As shown in Figure S31, α-Ni(OH)2/ CF and α-Ni(OH)2/CC merely demand overpotentials of 208 and 240 mV, respectively, to yield a current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] . The results show that the OER activity of α-Ni(OH)2/ CF is comparable to that of α-Ni(OH)2/NF. Moreover, the α- Ni(OH)2/CF and α-Ni(OH)2/CC self-standing electrodes also exhibit remarkable long-term stability at current densities of 10 and 100 mA cm[−][2] (Figure S31). The experimental results presented above confirm that electrodeposited α-Ni(OH)2 species on different conductive substrates all exhibit high electrocatalytic activity for OER, which can be attributed to the large active specific surface area as well as highly exposed catalytic active sites of the ultrathin nanosheets. 

## 4. CONCLUSIONS 

In summary, we have fabricated ultrathin nickel hydroxide nanosheets through a facile one-step electrodeposition method. The micro−nano structure as well as the active specific surface area and electron conductivity of the α- Ni(OH)2/NF self-supporting electrode can be optimized by adjusting the electrodeposition conditions, especially the electrodeposition potential, thereby greatly promoting its electrocatalytic activity for OER. Impressively, the α-Ni(OH)2/NF self-standing electrode exhibits excellent electrocatalytic performance for OER, requiring an extremely low overpotential of 192 mV to yield a current density of 10 mA cm[−][2] in 1.0 M KOH, being one of the best OER catalysts reported to date.

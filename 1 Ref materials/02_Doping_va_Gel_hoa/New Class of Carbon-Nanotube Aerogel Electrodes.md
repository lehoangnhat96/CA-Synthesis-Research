## **New Class of Carbon-Nanotube Aerogel Electrodes for Electrochemical Power Sources**** 

By _Tarik Bordjiba_ , _Mohamed Mohamedi_ ,* and _Lê H. Dao_ 

Carbon aerogels (CAGs) are novel ultralight materials with many interesting properties, such as low mass densities, continuous porosities, high surface areas, and high electrical conductivity. These properties are derived from the aerogel microstructure, which is a network of interconnected primary particles with characteristic diameters between 3 and 25 nm. Similar to carbon nanotubes (CNTs), the domain of applications of CAGs is enormous because of their unusual chemical and textural characteristics. For example, CAGs are promising materials for use as electrode materials for supercapacitors and rechargeable batteries, advanced catalyst supports for fuel cells, gas diffusion electrodes, adsorbents, chromatographic packing, thermal insulators, capacitive deionization, and molecular sieves, to name but a few. However, aerogels consist of agglomerate particles linked by covalent bridges (ladder structure). The contact between these particles and the space due to the pores unfortunately introduce a high internal resistance within the aerogel, which can be detrimental for electrochemical power source applications. In order to reduce the internal resistance, new strategies to introduce internal molecular nanocollectors (nanopathways for charges) such as CNTs absorbed in the porous matrix of the aerogel itself are being sought in our laboratory and elsewhere.[[1–5]] It is also expected that the high mechanical resilience typical of CNTs would further strengthen the mechanical strength of the CAG in several applications. 

Currently most aerogels and their composites are made by means of solution dispersion processes.[[4,6–11]] We have recently reported the synthesis of carbonized polyacrilonitrile–multiwalled carbon nanotube (MWNT) composite materials processed from their dispersion in dimethylformamide (DMF) and explored their use as electrodes for electrochemical capacitors.[[4]] The amount of MWNTs in the composite was varied from 3 to 12 wt %. The nanocomposites displayed high specific surface area (SSA) ranging between 670 and 710 m[2] g[–1] . A capacitance as high as 218 F g[–1] was obtained with an optimum composite containing 3 wt % MWNTs.

Larger amounts of the MWNTs diminished the effective portion of the active electrode material, resulting in the reduction of specific capacitance. Others have attempted reinforcement of CAGs with CNTs.[[1,3]] 

Dispersion preparation processes have proved rather difficult and still present considerable challenges, especially in the adhesion of nanotubes to the matrix material. Indeed, the effective utilization of CNTs in composite applications depends strongly on the ability to disperse the CNTs individually and uniformly throughout the host matrix without destroying their integrity or reducing their aspect ratio. The improvements that are required are better dispersion of CNTs within the aerogel matrix and better interfacial coating. 

The new concept we introduce in this Communication is shown in Scheme 1. We propose the use of CNTs directly grown onto a microfibrous carbon paper (MFCP) substrate. Afterwards, the CAG is synthesized on the CNTs. The flexibility afforded by this approach is the elimination of the need to control CNT concentration, thus allowing optimized CNT dispersion processes to be utilized for network reinforcement within the CAG. The CNT network is formed independently of the CAG, which makes the synthesis process very useful for making composites. 

A further important feature of these nanocomposites is their lack of binder. Indeed, it is well known that practical electrodes are made of the active material, a conductivity enhancer, and a binder for the adhesion of the particles. The use of binders and/or other adhesives may occlude the surface of the nanotubes or electrically isolate some particles, whereas the conductivity enhancers may interfere with the response of the active material considered. Our carbon nanotube aerogel (CNAG) composites are fabricated directly on the MFCP substrate without any binder or conductive additive. Indeed, the MFCP is electrically conductive and can act as the current collector. Furthermore the MFCP is a synthetic, thin, cheap, lightweight matrix with pore spaces on the micrometer scale, and it is corrosion resistant, which makes the CNAG nanocomposite of even greater interest as binderless electrodes for electrochemical power sources, for instance.

Raman spectra of the pristine CAG (for comparison), the pristine MWNTs (for comparison), and the CNAG samples shown in Figures 2b,e are shown in Figure 4. All the samples exhibit mainly two Raman bands, at around 1343 cm[–1] (D band) and 1583 cm[–1] (G band). The G band indicates original graphite features, whereas the D band is known to be related to disorder features of graphitic structures.[[17]] It is well known that the _R_ value, the relative intensity ratio of the D band to the G band, depends on both the degree of graphitization and the alignment of the graphitic planes of carbon materials.[[18]] Also, the _R_ value is sensitive to the ratio of the concentration of graphite edge planes and/or crystal boundaries to standard graphite planes, i.e., the lower the _R_ value, the greater the amount of sp[2] (graphite) clusters on the sample. The intensity ratios of the D band to the G band ( _I_ D/ _I_ G) were determined and were found to be 0.58, 0.999, 0.925, and 0.900 for CAG, MWNT, CNAG (of Fig. 2e), and CNAG (of Fig. 2b), respectively. These results indicate that the new CNAG specimens are disordered carbon.

Preliminary testing to evaluate the potential of these newly developed CNAG composites for supercapacitors was carried out in 5M KOH electrolyte. Figure 6 shows typical cyclic voltammograms (CVs) for a CNAG electrode recorded at 20 mV s[–1] scan rate. The CNAG electrode exhibited excellent capacitor behavior: the curve presents the typical rectangular shape expected for an ideal double-layer capacitor. Experiments at scan rates ranging from 5 to 100 mV s[–1] were performed and results showed that the current increases with increasing scan rate with no significant degeneration of the shape of the cyclic voltammograms at the high scan rates. It is known that specific capacitance can be estimated well from constant current (CC) charge–discharge measurements.[[22]] Thus, by means of CC charge– discharge experiments, it was found that CNAG delivered an outstanding high specific capacitance of 524 F g[–1] , almost four times that of CAG (134 F g[–1] ). To the best of our knowledge this is by far the highest capacitance for carbonaceous materials ever reported in the open literature. For comparison, the highest capacitance obtained with activated carbon was 320 F g[–1] .[[23]]

## _Experimental_ 

The synthesis of MWNTs on MFCP substrate was performed by chemical vapor deposition (CVD) and is reported elsewhere [12] The CNAG was prepared as follows: First polyacrylonitrile (PAN) powder was dissolved in a mixture of DMF and distilled water (84/16 mL) and the whole was heated until it boiled, at which point the aquagel was formed. Afterward, the MFCP/MWNT composite was dipped in the aquagel to allow this to deposit by coating. The resulting composite MFCP/MWNT/aquagel film was soaked overnight in acetone prior to the pretreatment and carbonization steps. Next, the resulting MFCP/ MWNT/aquagel was pretreated in air at 215 °C for 20 h. This important step led to cyclization reactions and to a ladder structure. Subsequently, the sample was carbonized at 850 °C for 8 h in an inert atmosphere (argon), leading to the CNAG. Then, the CNAG samples are activated under carbon dioxide treatment using the following soak cycle. First the sample was heated from ambient temperature to 800 °C (over 1 h) then held at 800 °C under argon flow for 45 min. The atmo-
